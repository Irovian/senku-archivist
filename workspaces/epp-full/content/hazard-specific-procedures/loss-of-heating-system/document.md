# Loss of Heating System

## Purpose
This procedure describes how {{facility_name}} responds when the heating system is unavailable, unreliable, or unable to maintain safe resident, staff, and building conditions.

## Recognition and Activation
Loss of heat may result from heating equipment failure, boiler failure, fuel interruption, electric service loss, water service loss, severe weather, building damage, or other utility disruption. Staff should report cold resident areas, heating alarms, equipment shutdowns, drafts, frozen or damaged pipes, or resident comfort concerns immediately.

The command center or assigned leader should determine whether the event requires temperature monitoring, resident relocation within the building, alternate heat, vendor support, census reduction, evacuation, regulatory notification, or activation of related electric, generator, gas, water, or boiler/fuel procedures.

Notification to {{regulatory_agency_name}} or another authority should occur when required by the event, duration, resident impact, indoor temperature, evacuation risk, or facility policy.

## Heating System Assessment
{{maintenance_resource_owner_title}} should assess the heating source, expected outage duration, affected zones, weather conditions, fuel supply, electric or gas dependencies, boiler or hot water impacts, ventilation settings, and building envelope problems such as doors or windows that do not seal.

The facility heating system is described as {{heating_system_description}}. Temperature monitoring should include {{temperature_monitoring_locations}}. The minimum safe temperature threshold or escalation standard is {{minimum_safe_temperature_threshold}}.

{{boiler_fuel_responsibility_description}} should be reviewed when heating-system loss affects boilers, fuel supply, domestic hot water, or related building operations.

## Resident Protection and Temperature Control
Departments should close windows and doors, report air leaks, conserve heat, move residents away from cold areas, provide appropriate blankets or clothing, and monitor residents who are vulnerable to cold stress.

The command center or assigned leader should evaluate whether occupied areas can remain safe, whether residents can be consolidated into warmer areas, and whether residents with high clinical vulnerability need relocation, transfer, or additional monitoring.

Alternate heat sources, vendors, or warmed areas include {{alternate_heat_sources}}. Temporary heat should be used only when approved by maintenance, leadership, and any required fire or life-safety authority.

## Department Coordination
Nursing should monitor resident condition, room temperatures, and clinical impacts. Maintenance should coordinate heating repairs, utility/vendor contact, safe equipment operation, and building-system checks. Other departments should support resident movement, blankets, clothing, meal delivery, and conservation measures as directed.

If heat loss is caused by electric service, generator failure, gas/propane loss, or water service loss, the related procedure should also be activated.

## Recovery
Before normal operations resume, maintenance should confirm that heating systems, boilers, pumps, valves, thermostats, ventilation equipment, and affected building areas are safe and functioning. Departments should continue temperature checks until occupied areas remain stable.

Remaining damage, vendor actions, resident-care impacts, relocation decisions, supply use, and lessons learned should be documented for recovery.

## What Staff Should Understand
- Report loss of heat or unsafe temperatures immediately.
- Resident safety depends on both indoor temperature and resident vulnerability.
- Alternate heat must be approved and safely operated.
- Electric, gas, water, and boiler problems may each cause or worsen heat loss.
- Fuel-supply, gas-service, and electric-service details remain in their dedicated procedures or resource lists.
